    Rules differ by country: examples from the United States and India
    United States
    Hiring must not discriminate (EEOC and state laws). Background checks follow specific consent and notice rules. Some places regulate AI used to screen candidates, such as New York City's bias audit law. Some states require pay ranges in job ads. Employment is mostly at will.
    India
    Hiring practice follows central and state law and company policy. Candidate data falls under the DPDP Act, so keep only what you need. Background and reference checks need the person's consent. Some states limit hiring rules for local residents, and the new labour codes are being brought in, so check what applies.

    These are starting pointers for comparing the two, not legal advice. Find the equivalent rules for your own country. Rules change and vary by state, company size and contract, so check the current text.

    How this is calculated

    AI helps with every task you do not pick as Human-led. An activity's hours are shared across its tasks by weights. We set the starting weights by hand, and you can change them. Tasks you add yourself all count the same. The big percentage is the share of time with AI involved. Each of the four boxes shows that label's share of the time, with the time itself and the number of tasks underneath. The same percentage is used on every process, so they can be compared. A tie goes to Human-led. Activities you ignore are not counted anywhere. It counts time, not job numbers. The hours are starting guesses for a general recruiter, not industry figures. Time spent checking AI's work is not taken off, so read the AI hours as a best case. A small change to a weight can move a result a little.

    A caution. This is how it looks today. It comes from a first view of each activity and the linked sources, not from measuring your own process. Treat the task choices and hours as opinions to test. Before relying on any of it, try a small trial, check results for fairness across groups, tell candidates when AI is used, and confirm local law. Ask five recent candidates and two hiring managers what they would change before you rely on any of this. This is not legal advice.

    Before using AI with candidates
    1. Talk to your lawyer.
    2. Check local data protection rules. Many places ask for extra care with sensitive personal data.
    3. Check local rules on AI in employment. Some places treat AI used for hiring, pay, promotion or ending work as high-risk, or require bias audits and notices to people.
    4. Ask your tool providers. How do they test for bias, and what data do they keep?

    These are pointers, not legal advice. Rules change, so check the current text.

    What the recruiter's role could become ideas to discuss
    • Skills that grow: talking with managers and candidates, judging evidence, checking AI output and asking why, looking after fairness, designing the process.
    • Skills that fade: copying data between systems, manual scheduling, first-pass sorting, drafting routine messages.
    • Roles that could appear: hiring advisor, candidate guide, fairness steward, process designer.
    • Questions to answer: what will the freed hours be used for, who needs new skills and when, and how will candidates and managers say whether it helped?

    These are prompts, not predictions. Your own team may see it differently.
